All 31 ways to tile a floor with regular polygons so that every corner
looks like every other, or every corner looks like one of two kinds: the 11
Archimedean tilings and the 20 two-uniform tilings.

## What it is

Take triangles, squares, hexagons, octagons and dodecagons, all with sides
of the same length, and fit them edge to edge with no gaps. Walk round any
corner and write down the polygons you pass: `3.4.6.4` means a triangle, a
square, a hexagon and a square. In the **Archimedean** tilings every corner
reads the same, and that short name pins down the whole pattern. There are
exactly eleven. In the **two-uniform** tilings the corners come in exactly
two kinds, such as `[3⁶; 3².4.3.4]`, and there are exactly twenty. Where two
tilings share both corner names they are told apart as (1) and (2).

Each page shows one tiling, coloured by polygon shape, by symmetry class or
so that no two neighbours match. It can show the **Laves dual** instead,
the tiling you get by joining the centres of the polygons around each
corner, and it can mark each corner by its kind. The name is printed
underneath with proper exponents.

## How to use it

Colour it, frame it, or teach with it. A run of pages makes a classroom
wall chart of all 31 tilings. Ask students to walk round a corner and read
off its name, check that the angles at each corner add up to 360°, or find
the two kinds of corner on a two-uniform page (turn on the corner marks to
check). Line art makes calm geometric colouring pages. Set the edge length
to a quilting size, such as 72 points for 1-inch sides, and every polygon
on the page is a template for English paper piecing: print, cut along the
lines, and tack fabric over the papers.

## Purpose

Tilings by regular polygons are where geometry lessons on angles, symmetry
and pattern meet, and they are the starting point for quilting, tiling and
Islamic-pattern design. The lane already has Islamic, Penrose and Cairo
tilings and wallpaper groups. This adds the complete classical catalogue,
drawn exactly and labelled with its standard names.

## History

Johannes Kepler described the regular and semiregular tilings in
*Harmonices Mundi* (1619). The eleven were rediscovered in the twentieth
century, and Duncan Sommerville (1905) and Otto Krötenheerdt (1969)
enumerated the k-uniform tilings. Krötenheerdt proved there are exactly 20
two-uniform tilings. Grünbaum and Shephard's *Tilings and Patterns* (1987)
set the modern notation. The duals are named after the crystallographer
Fritz Laves, who used them to describe crystal structures.

## This implementation

- **Spec knobs:** `tiling` (`random` by default, or a name such as
  `3.4.6.4`, `4.8^2`, `3^6; 3^2.4.3.4` or `3^6; 3^4.6 (2)`), `family`
  (what a random pick draws from: `any`, `archimedean`, `two_uniform`),
  `edge` (polygon side in points, default 34), `dual` (the Laves dual),
  `colouring` (`shape`, `orbit`, `proper`), `palette` (`jewel`, `pastel`,
  `earth`, `mono`), `line_art`, `stroke`, `label` (print the name, default
  on), `mark_vertices` (a black or white dot for each kind of corner),
  `width`, `height`, `margin`.
- **Generation:** each tiling is a row of a data table: two lattice
  vectors and the polygons of one translation cell. Every point is exact,
  eight integers giving `x` and `y` as `(a + b√2 + c√3 + d√6)/4`, the field
  in which all corners of these tilings lie. A polygon is stored as its
  side count, first corner and first edge direction (a multiple of 15°),
  and its corners are generated by walking unit edges and turning by
  `360°/n`. The cells were extracted from published illustrations of the
  tilings, snapped to exact coordinates and cut down to one translation
  cell. Two of them were built by construction: `[3⁶; 3².6²]` (triangles
  with hexagons at two of every nine lattice points) and `[4.6.12; 3.4.6.4]`
  (`3.4.6.4` with the hexagon-and-ring patches on an index-4 sublattice
  merged into dodecagons). A page lays enough translates to cover the
  frame, starting from a seeded offset, and clips them to it. Colouring by
  `shape` gives each polygon size a colour, and tilings with a single shape
  (`3⁶`, `4⁴`, `6³`, and all Laves duals of Archimedean tilings) fall back
  to a proper colouring. `orbit` colours by tile symmetry class. `proper`
  is greedy in reading order over shared edges. A dual face joins the
  centres of the polygons around each complete corner.
- **Solving:** nothing to solve. This is a design.
- **Guarantees (tested, all 31 tilings):** every polygon closes exactly and
  every edge has length exactly one, proved in exact arithmetic in
  ℚ(√2, √3). In a patch, the corner angles of the central cell sum to
  exactly 360° (integer arithmetic in 15° units), every edge is shared by
  exactly two polygons, and the polygons' area equals the cell's.
  Translating by either lattice vector maps the corners onto corners. The
  corner configurations found are exactly the named ones, and the printed
  name spells them. A brute-force symmetry search (every rotation by 15°,
  with and without reflection, with each translation that carries a corner
  to a corner) finds exactly one corner orbit for the Archimedean tilings
  and two for the others, plus the tile and edge orbit counts of the
  published table. There are 11 and 20 entries, and no two share all their
  invariants. The Laves duals of the Archimedean tilings have congruent
  faces. Proper colourings never repeat across an edge. Line-art pages pass
  the adult colourability gate. Every page's meta lists the corner
  configurations it shows (`configurations_checked`), the orbit counts and
  the symmetry order.
- **Caveats:** for `[3⁶; 3⁴.6]` (2) the computed edge-orbit count is 6,
  while some published tables give 7. The tile count (5), corner orbits (2)
  and symmetry (p6) match. `[3^6; 3^2.6^2]` as built has mirror symmetry
  (p6m). Both are reported as computed.
